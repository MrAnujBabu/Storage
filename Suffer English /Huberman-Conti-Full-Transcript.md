## 00:00 — Introduction: The Self & Its Malleability

**Andrew Huberman:** We all have a **self-concept** — a name, a sense of who we are, what bothers us, what excites us. The question I've been living with for a long time is: **how malleable is our self-view?** If we're not completely happy with our relationship to ourselves, how much **flexibility** is there?

**Dr. Paul Conti:** I think it's **very malleable**. I think there's a lot of flexibility — but we have to be **willing to look at ourselves**. Very often we're *not* looking at ourselves. We're **afraid of what we're going to find**, or we don't know *how* to understand or how to bring change — so we don't look, and then we see ourselves as inflexible and stuck. But if we're willing to look, and we bring **==compassionate curiosity==** to ourselves — *"Hey, what can I learn about myself? What might I be interested in changing or emphasizing?"* — we can bring a **lot of change**.

## 02:51 — 🔧 Tool: "What's Going Right?" — State Dependence

**Huberman:** The title of your book — *"What's Going Right"* — is that a good **lens** to start looking through when we examine ourselves?

**Conti:** I think starting with what's going right isn't just a way of looking at things because it *feels* better — it's **consistent with truth**. There is **far more going right** in any of us than going wrong. And it's a good place to start because it helps us look at what's *not* going the way we want — **but we should start from a position of strength.**

> [!danger] The Mental Health System Problem
> "The mental health system really tells us to look at ourselves in the **opposite** way — to look at ourselves through **what is going wrong** and to put **labels** on ourselves that often just make us feel **worse**, or **helpless**, or **hopeless**. But if we start with what's going right and we bring **curiosity**, then there are processes we can follow to understand and bring **real change**."

### 🔧 Tool: Examine Your Self-Talk

**Conti:** A good place to start is **looking at your self-talk**. What are you saying to yourself in **quiet moments** when no one else is listening? What **messages** are you giving yourself? Often we're telling ourselves things — negative or critical — and we're **not even aware** that we're saying them over and over.

### 🔧 Tool: Examine Your Life Narrative

Another strategy is to think about the **life narrative** you're telling yourself. If you tell someone about *you*, what do you say in a **reflexive** way? Does it match what's **real and true** about your life?

> [!tip] Two Foundational Pillars
> We all share a **structure of self** and a **function of self**. These are foundational pillars — where we can look to understand ourselves better and bring better health.

### 🔧 Tool: Understand State Dependence

**Huberman:** Some people are incredibly **state-dependent** — when they're with someone, they think and feel completely differently than when they're alone. Almost like it's **two different lives**.

**Conti:** For most of us, life is moving very **fast** and has a lot of **stressors**. We become very state-dependent instead of being able to **observe ourselves**. To be able to observe ourselves is how we **knit together one self across situations**. This is sometimes called an **==observing ego==** — and this is how we can both be state-dependent *and* have a self that is **true across all of those states**.

## 12:44 — 🔧 Tool: Compassionate Curiosity — Falseness & Social Media

**Huberman:** When and **how** should someone sit down to think about their strengths, their self-talk, what they're made of?

**Conti:** All we need to bring is **curiosity**. Curiosity doesn't have to be overly serious or worried. It can be **light-hearted**. There are so many things we're curious about in the world — but so often, what we **leave out** is being curious about **ourselves**.

> [!question] Key Questions to Ask Yourself
> - What runs through **everything** I do?
> - How is it that I feel so **different** doing one thing than another?
> - What are the **common threads** of *me* that run throughout my life?
> - When am I presenting a **true and honest** self?
> - When am I presenting a **false self** that even *I* know is false?

### On Social Media & Falseness of Self

**Conti:** If a person is engaged in something that even *they themselves* know isn't real — wanting everyone to see what's **best** in their life, maybe to **hide** something — there's a good place for **compassionate curiosity** about the **falseness** of that. *"What am I trying to protect against? Why do I want people to see me differently from how my life actually is?"*

> [!important] The Key Principle
> "The important point is always to be **honest with ourselves**. And if we bring **compassionate curiosity**, then we're **not mad at ourselves** — we're not coming at ourselves with *'What's wrong with me?'* or *'Why can't I do this better?'* We guide ourselves toward **honesty and truth**."

**Huberman:** We are in a **strange new version of humanity**. Someone sitting by themselves today has a vastly different experience than 30 years ago because they're getting information about what *other people* are doing. The moment we think what we're doing **needs to be shared**, it **changes the experience**. It's no longer truly an **alone** experience.

**Conti:** There's a **sweet spot** of connectedness. We know isolation isn't good — but the modern world has gone too far the **opposite** direction: **not enough aloneness**. When we're overconnected, we look for **external cues** to decide what we like, how we feel. We need enough **aloneness** that we're still thinking about ourselves and the questions of life **on our own** before we ping outside for information, validation, or guidance.

## 21:00 — Doing vs. Thinking — Self-Reflection

**Huberman:** Marc Andreessen made a provocative statement: *"Great men of history didn't sit around thinking about their thoughts."* However tongue-in-cheek, it raises a useful question: **how much thinking vs. how much doing** when exploring the self? We don't want to spiral into a tunnel we can't get out of — but we also need to put things out into the world.

**Conti:** It depends very much on **who that person is** and where they need to face to **break new ground** of self. I think it's **unfortunate** that most people would find the idea of just **being with themselves** to be anxiety-provoking. I think that comes from a **lack of leadership** in the mental health field and from the **stigma** of mental health — those **black box fears** of what we don't understand. What we don't understand is **ourselves**.

> [!quote] Reflection vs. Action
> "There are some people who are very **assertive** — they want high levels of **doing** in the world, but they still need some **reflection**. There are others who are very **reflective** and doing less. We need to understand **what profile works** for each person. If we're **well-balanced** — asserting ourselves at levels that work for us and finding **pleasure and gratification** in healthy ways — now we're finding balance."

**Too much doing, not enough reflection** → diminishing returns, unsatisfied.
**Too little doing, too much reflection** → idle, learned helplessness.

### On People Who Don't Reflect Much

**Huberman:** Are there people who just don't think about their thinking very much? They just *do* stuff. Are they missing out?

**Conti:** Even though we have different **natural levels of reflective inclination**, the question is: **Are they happy? Are their lives going well?** If life is going well — healthy, secure relationships — and they're not reflecting very much... that sounds **good**. I would characterize that as living through the **==generative drive==** — being productive, contributory people in the world, making the world better, learning, growing. That's a **great way to be**.

> [!tip] Key Insight
> "For **most of us**, in order to get there, we *do* have to be **reflective**. Our brains will **lead us to reflection** — because they're saying, *'Hey, we need to stop and think about things. That's how we're going to make better decisions.'* But if we're moving so fast or we're **defended against it**, then we're not reflective — and that's **not good** for us."

## 29:55 — External vs. Internal Processing — Finding Balance

**Huberman:** Some people are **external processors** — they need to *talk* things through to understand. Others are **internal processors** — they work things out by sitting, walking, driving, thinking. Are these semi-separate bins of people?

**Conti:** I don't know that they're separate bins. What happens often is we get **stuck in our own minds** — thinking, but not thinking *productively*, because we're stuck in our own **loops**. When we take the thought process **outside of us** — by writing words down or saying them to another person — we're bringing **different brain processes online**, different **error-checking** processes.

> [!tip] Why External Processing Works
> "Making words and putting those words out there — in writing or in speech — is **different**. It holds the brain **more accountable**. That's why sometimes you'll say something out loud and realize, *'Oh, I figured that out!'* Or someone just **listens** and you feel helped — because just by being there, the other person is **forming words**, and you do more **due diligence** inside yourself that way."

### The Power of Collaborative Processing

**Huberman:** I had a conversation with my sister — for two-thirds of it we weren't aligned. But then we hit this **overlap in the Venn diagram** — real **synergy**. I couldn't have gotten there without the **friction** of what she was bringing forward.

**Conti:** You're doing something **together** that involves **real and open communication**. You have to say, *"This is how I think and feel"* — put that out there, **test it**, bounce it off the other person, and take inside what *they* think. It's a complicated process — how human beings come to understand one another, come to agree, or find a way forward **even without complete agreement**. We have to do these things **outside of us**.

> [!tip] The Balance
> "Most often, if we're going to be at our **healthiest**, we want to be able to do **some of it inside** — a good place to start. And we bring our **best vetted self** to what we find outside of us. That may be collaboration with another person — coming to middle ground when there isn't agreement. If we start with ourselves and bring **self-understanding** to the fore, we're much **stronger** — not in forcing our way through things, but in terms of both **self-knowledge** and **flexibility** when meeting other people."

## 42:26 — Quiet vs. Verbal — Questions to Learn About Self

**Huberman:** There's an assumption that if people are **talking a lot**, there's a lot going on inside. And if people are **quiet**, either they're more regulated or there's not much going on. But my graduate advisor Barbara Chapman was described perfectly: **"She's quiet, but not shy."** She communicated **judiciously** — high signal-to-noise.

**Conti:** We have to be **very wary** of mapping a stereotype — *"this is good, that's not good"* — and applying a value system when we're outside of looking at a person **in context**. Being internalized, speaking less, being hyperverbal — they could mean **anything under the sun**. It has to be: **who is the person** and **what is the context**?

> [!tip] Context Matters
> - Someone who speaks less in meetings but communicates **judiciously** → adaptive, organized, deliberate
> - Someone who is hyperverbal with **constructive ideas** and enthusiasm → great
> - Someone who is hyperverbal but **anxious**, repeating things, seeking validation → different
>
> "**The person and the context makes all the difference.** We're human, so we fit patterns — but we're all **unique**, so you won't know what pattern someone is fitting until you **really look** at them."

## 53:17 — The Examined Life & Reflection — Changing Unwanted Behavior

**Huberman:** One thing I love about your book is the **probe questions** — questions for people to ask themselves to explore the self. That's a huge gift. We have so much *"What's going wrong? What's wrong with me?"* — but starting from knowing **what questions to ask oneself** is immensely useful.

### 🔧 Tool: Asking the "Why" Question

**Huberman:** To gain more **agency** over any area of our life, we have to ask the **why** question. *Why am I doing what I'm doing? Why aren't I doing the thing that would serve me better?*

**Conti:** If someone is just reporting a **laundry list** of what they do and how they feel — the way to probe is to encourage **reflection**. I wonder: **how much of that are you really choosing?** How much is **intentional** vs. **reflex**?

> [!example] Real-Life Change Through Examination
> "By midway through the second day of an intensive process, the person wants to **revisit almost everything**. They realize that only 10-20% of the things they said they do are things they **really value** and want to do *more* of. The other 80%? They're **not so sure**. They don't know *why* they're doing those things. Now we're really along the **process of change** — because we're **looking at ourselves**."

**Huberman:** So in order to gain **agency**, we examine our lives. But what if someone says, *"This is just what I do. This is just life."* — how do you get them to look?

**Conti:** I tell them: **"You're showing us where the X's are."** The X marks the spot — to dig. If every time you go out with someone you come home feeling **drained** and **hopeless** — there's **treasure** there. Let's dig. Let's understand **why** you're doing it. There's always something to learn.

## 01:02:54 — Making Positive Changes — Problem Solving

### 🔧 Tool: Collaborative Action Planning

**Huberman:** Do you give people **specific action directives**? Like, *"Go to the gym twice this week"*?

**Conti:** Sometimes — but it's **much more effective** if it's arrived at **collaboratively**. We talk back and forth. Maybe the person wants to go to the gym *five* times, but every time they try that much, they get **frustrated** and don't go at all.

> [!tip] Small Wins Empower
> "How about something more **measured**? Maybe once or twice. If you get **once** under your belt, you can get **twice** the next week. We don't want someone to think the process should occur so **fast** that it can't possibly occur that fast — and then they get **frustrated** in two weeks. We set **reasonable expectations** and put one foot in front of the other. **Small wins empower and embolden** us to take a little more chances and get **bigger wins**."

### 🔧 Tool: Bolster Yourself — Set Up for Success

**Conti:** We help you **bolster yourself** so all your **arrows are going in the same direction**. You set yourself up for **success** — you don't try to go to the gym the morning after a long night out. You get a **win**. And that win builds **empowerment** and **agency** — the sense that *"I can do these things I've decided to do."*

## 01:09:03 — Behavior Pattern Insight — Reclaiming Agency

**Huberman:** At what point does it make sense to think about the **patterns** you were exposed to as a child — as a way of having more agency?

**Conti:** It makes sense **right now**. **Insight sets us free** and it puts us in the **driver's seat** of our lives. Otherwise, we're just **reacting**.

### Pattern Repetition vs. Reaction — Both Need Insight

**Conti:** Imagine a person who had a very **over-controlling parent**. Without insight, they might:
1. **Repeat the pattern** — become over-controlling themselves (they associate control with being powerful, feeling less vulnerable)
2. **Push to the opposite** — become *too* permissive, not exercising the healthy control that *does* make sense

> [!tip] The Insight Wedge
> "It's **insight** that lets us say: *'Oh, I see what that was in my past. I can look at it — good, bad, or otherwise — and then I can **decide**: How do I want to integrate that information so the **whole me** is going to be in the driver's seat?'* Often early childhood experiences have a **great impact** upon us, and like **automatons** we're acting one way or another — and we **don't know why**. But **insight** lets us gain the understanding. Here's how it was when I was growing up. I can **look at it**. I can **see it**. And then I can **choose**."

## 01:17:06 — Agency & Control — Getting in Your Own Way

**Huberman:** What is that **wedge** that lets people change their behavior simply by **understanding** that some or all of it is inherited from a pattern? What *is* insight?

**Conti:** When we realize that something — whether external or internal — is **controlling us**, it **defuses that tension**. Part of why is because **none of us want to be controlled**. We don't like to be like the Manchurian Candidate — triggered and then behaving **automatically**. We really **don't like that**.

> [!tip] The Enemy Is Not You
> "If I realize *'Gosh, I've been programmed — and when someone disagrees with me, it makes me feel so bad or vulnerable, like I felt when I was a kid — and now I'm being just like my parent was'*... that realization is powerful. **Who's most likely to thwart my efforts toward being healthier? It's absolutely me.** But it doesn't mean I'm my **enemy**. If I'm standing in my own way, there's a **reason**."

### 🔧 Tool: Get on the Same Page with Yourself

**Conti:** Get the person to be on their **own side**. Instead of *"I want to do A, B, and C but I just can't"* — shift to: *"Whoa, do I really **want** to do it? If I do, what's **keeping me** from doing it? How am **I** keeping me from doing it?"*

> [!quote] The Essence of Agency
> "Now we bring our **gumption** — our resources, internally and externally — to the problem, and the **whole thing shifts**. The person is on the **same page** with themselves. They're not making themselves feel **worse** by wanting to go and not going. They understand **why**. And now we're at that **simple goodness principle** — we're all on the same page with ourselves, and we accomplish our goals."

**Huberman:** That's the essence of **agency**. You have to be on **your own side**. Realizing *"I'm being controlled — even though I think I'm controlling myself"* — **BOOM**. Behavior changes. Somebody can **advocate for themselves**.

## 01:22:49 — Trauma, Living Intentionally — Internal Turmoil

**Huberman:** How do people who had **reasonably healthy or trauma-free childhoods** operate? Are they moving toward things from a **genuine place of curiosity** — not pushing off anything, not countering a childhood example?

**Conti:** Yes — in the sense that I would map that to **==living intentionally==** — being as **self-aware** as we can be while also realizing we can't be *completely* self-aware, and then living intentionally. That's what we're trying to get to.

> [!important] Trauma Makes It Harder — But Not Impossible
> "The presence of **real trauma** that overwhelms our coping skills does make it **harder** to achieve these things — which is why we want to look at trauma if it exists. But it doesn't **prevent** it. People with significant traumas can still get on this path and have insight into how trauma is affecting them."

### 🔧 Tool: Dealing with Internal Turmoil — "I Get Tired Just Thinking About It"

**Huberman:** What about someone who says, *"I get tired just thinking about it"* — like going to the gym?

**Conti:** That tells me there's a lot of **brain space** and a lot of **energy** taken up in the *thinking* of it. For a lot of people, **thinking about trying** to go to the gym takes more energy than **actually being there** — because it's running around in their head: how they failed, how bad they'll feel, whether they'll do it. They're making something very **complicated**.

> [!tip] Simplify
> "That's a **marker** — there's something going on that we want to get at because it's not the healthiest process. The statement *'I get tired just thinking about it'* represents **10 mental workouts** with **no improvement in physical health**. Let's take those 10 mental workouts and figure out: **how can we turn that into ONE physical workout?** That person's going to feel a lot better — physically **and** mentally."

## 01:29:08 — 🔧 Tool: Intrusive Thoughts — Self-Talk Awareness & Dreams

### On Self-Talk

**Huberman:** Is it true that *however you talk to others, that's also how you talk to yourself*?

**Conti:** **Sometimes yes, sometimes no.** You have to look at the **person** and the **situation**. For *most* people, when there's a difference, it's the **opposite** — they treat others much **better** than they treat themselves.

> [!warning] The Negative Self-Talk Trap
> "We'll give other people a kind word or benefit of the doubt — but then inside we go: *'What's wrong with me? I'm an idiot. I messed that up again.'* There's a **lot** of that going on inside of us. If you're going to make yourself special, don't make yourself special in a **negative way**. Other people get a pass — but for us, we use much **harsher language**."

**Conti on the person who "gives stress" instead of receiving it:** "For most people, if someone is treating others poorly and making them unhappy while feeling okay inside — that's a **different kind of problem** and it's **not common**. Usually, the person who is being unkind to others has real **turmoil** inside."

### 🔧 Tool: Dealing with Intrusive Thoughts

**Step 1: Identify Them**
Some people have intrusive thoughts — things they say to themselves **hundreds of times a day** — and they're **not aware** of it. Until they stop and think: *What am I saying to myself over and over? What's running around?*

**Step 2: Understand the Purpose**
If I keep telling myself *"nothing's going to be okay"* — **why** am I saying that?
- Am I so **afraid** that I'm trying to save myself from the **shock** of things not being okay?
- Am I **afraid** because something happened in the past and I haven't **processed** it?

> [!tip] Intrusive Thoughts Have Meaning
> "**There is a meaning to intrusive thoughts. There always is.** We want to recognize them, look for that meaning, and then apply strategies."

**Step 3: Apply Strategies**
- **Thought redirection** — sometimes we think something because we're thinking it over and over; redirecting gives us greater **control**
- **Diffuse the energy** — understand *why* we're thinking it and take measures
- **Change the situation** — if I'm worried about safety, maybe I'm in an **unsafe situation** that I need to change
- **Medicine** — sometimes medications can help

### On Dreams

**Huberman:** Are our dreams **informative**?

**Conti:** People's dreams can have a **lot of meaning**, regardless of what kind of thinker they are. Someone who's a very **concrete thinker** may have dreams that are *especially* telling us a lot — because what the **unconscious mind** wants to bring to the surface doesn't have a lot of other room to do that.

> [!tip] Curiosity About Dreams
> "The one factor is being **curious about ourselves** — because then we tend to **remember more** what went on inside us during sleep. Being curious about what our brains are telling us during sleep can be **very helpful**. But we have to be **careful** and **respectful** of our own complexity. Sometimes dreams don't have meaning we can clearly discern. But if we approach them **level-headed**, it can be **remarkable** what dreams can tell us — something can come out **allegorically**, capturing events that unfolded across years in a very simple way."

## 01:35:23 — Trauma & Emotions — Healing Childhood Trauma

**Huberman:** You've said that **anxiety, trauma, and stress don't know the clock or the calendar**. Time is **erased**. The negative feelings seem like they'll go on **forever** — which is why they're so scary.

**Conti:** You can say *"that was then, this is now"* — but your **limbic system doesn't care**. The logic systems are telling you it's in the past because the **clock** says so — but the **emotion systems** have a very different reality. **A trigger in the now can make *then* become *now*.**

> [!tip] The String Metaphor
> "In the logical systems, time is like a steel rod going in one direction. But in the **limbic systems**, it's like a **string** — and something just made you feel **right now** exactly the way you felt 10 years ago, and it **put the two parts of the string together**. That's **real**. And it's telling you: *'Go dig there — because that is not just in the past. Emotionally, it is still in your present.'*"

### 🔧 Tool: Healing Childhood Trauma with Compassionate Curiosity

**Conti:** The most efficient way is bringing **compassionate curiosity** to ourselves — where we look at our past **without having a dog in the fight**. We don't have to see it a certain way.

> [!warning] Common Distortions
> Some people will **minimize** what happened — because they feel they won't be okay if they see how bad it was.
> Others will **maximize** or **dial it up** — trying to anchor to things in their life now that they're not happy with.
> Both bring so much **emotion** that we can't look with **equanimity**.

> [!tip] The Process
> "If I can look at my past with **calmness of mind** and see the **realness** of how it's affected me — now I can start talking about **malleability** and make **progress**. We have to be able to look at ourselves. Very often we don't want to because we bring **fear and criticism** instead of **compassion**. But if we can just **observe** ourselves — now we can get in touch with what *did* happen, and what we're making of it *now*. Then maybe we put those words outside of us — in writing, in speech, to a trusted other, or to a therapist."

### 🔧 Tool: Look at What's Going Right (Not Just What's Wrong)

**Huberman:** Do people look back and think about **good things** that happened to them often enough?

**Conti:** **No.** We tend to have a **bias toward the negative**. We don't stop and think: *"I did that really well,"* or *"It didn't come out the way I wanted, but I learned from it,"* or *"I really tried."* We don't do that — and this bias means we start making the **stories of ourselves about the negative**.

> [!quote] The Opposite Is True
> "If we look at what's gone well — our successes, even things that weren't successes from the outside but where we **grew** and **learned** — then we are **bolstering ourselves**, **empowering ourselves**. We wouldn't become **complacent**. We would become **happier**, **healthier**, **more effective** in our lives."

## 01:43:32 — Photographs, Positive Climate for the Mind — Spirituality, Good & Evil

### 🔧 Tool: Surround Yourself with Positive Memories

**Huberman:** Memory researcher Larry Squire had photographs on his office wall of good times. He said: **"Even if you don't look at them deliberately each day, having them surrounding you creates a vessel of awesome memories."** He studied explicit and implicit memory — and even **implicit** registration of positive photos primes your emotional states.

**Conti:** What he's talking about is being able to have **control over the climate within us**. The **unconscious mind** sets parameters for us — it's the **climate** in which we're living.

> [!tip] Pre-Program a Positive Bias
> "If our climate is being **programmed toward the negative** — because we're thinking about what we did wrong, what we should have done differently, what's going to go wrong — then we're biasing the unconscious mind to throw the **negative answer** to the surface. But if you surround yourself with **real positive memories**, you're **pre-programming a bias toward the positive**. It's not a false bias — those memories are **real**. He is **priming** the unconscious mind to see the **positive side** of things."

### On Spirituality, God, and Evil

**Huberman:** If the best way to change behavior is to be on **your own side** — and the best way to be on your own side is to **not be controlled** by something else — does the God/Devil framework help?

**Conti:** From a psychological perspective — **yes and no**. If we get too **reductionist** — a single force of good and a single force of evil — we tend to **over-identify**. Either I want to be the good force but I can't be good enough and now I feel evil — or I feel the evil in the world is directed at me. We personify good and evil and either over-identify or feel beleaguered.

> [!tip] The Nuanced View
> "If we think in a **broader way** — consistent with major spiritual traditions — we see there are **forces for good** and **pushes toward good** in the universe around us and within us. And there are forces toward what is **not good** — toward looking the other way, for example. Not pure evil. Most of us aren't going to step on someone when they're down — but could we be **tempted to look the other way**? If we see the **subtlety and nuance** to how good and evil play out, we're viewing ourselves and the world much more **consistently with reality**."

## 01:52:53 — Happiness & Expectations — Death, Living a Good Life

### Is "Happy-Go-Lucky" a Reasonable Goal?

**Huberman:** Can I aspire to be **happy-go-lucky** and also be a productive person?

**Conti:** Unfortunately, **no**. Happy-go-lucky implies that there's **not an awareness** that there are difficult things in the world — and in your own life. I don't think you can be happy-go-lucky, and I think it's **good** that you can't — because who wants to lose the **grounding** of the things that are real?

> [!tip] True Happiness = Peace + Contentment + Delight
> "I absolutely believe that you can be **happy** — and what's better than happiness includes **some turning away** or **forgetfulness**. What we want is:
> 1. **Peace** — to just *be*, without so much going on inside or coming at us
> 2. **Contentment** — awareness of our lives, the things that went well *and* didn't, and feeling good about the **whole arc**
> 3. **The capacity for delight** — we all had it as children, and if we don't have it now as adults, there's something we can do about it. We all need to be able to see something that just makes us **light up**."

**Conti on embracing fate:** "Can we embrace the fate we've created for ourselves and want to live it **over and over again** — even knowing the things in it that may be tragic? **Yes.** We can find **peace**, **contentment**, and **delight** — and weave them into our lives."

### On Living a Good Life — Insights from the Elderly

**Huberman:** Have you encountered someone close to the end of their life who really **nailed it** — who genuinely felt good about how they spent their mental life and energies?

**Conti:** A family member — very old, would be 120 now — who had made something of himself with **no education**. He started a bank, it became international, he gave back to the community. He'd had real **tragedies** — lost a child. When he learned I was going to medical school, he asked to see me. He told me he was **happy with his life**, realized he could die at any moment, and **accepted** that. He tried to do his best, made something of himself — and there was sadness, things he wished were different — but he was **happy** and **okay with dying**.

> [!quote] The Warning
> "He wanted me to know that it was **tempting** to put so much pressure on yourself that you could achieve a lot and **not be able to feel good about it**. It's not something I forgot. I think of it with fair frequency. That person was clearly describing being able to have **peace** and **contentment** — to feel good about his life even knowing the things that were not great — and still the **capacity for delight**. Things he was still very **excited** about, his face would still **light up**."

### On Living an Examined Life

**Conti:** "We have to be living an **examined life** in order to live **intentionally**. Yes, we do have to look at ourselves — but the good news is that's **okay**. Most of us don't want to be dragged kicking and screaming to looking at ourselves — but that's just because we're **afraid**. If we know *'I'm not going to find anything there that's going to really shock me'* — and there's a **process** I can go through — if I look at myself, I can use the knowledge to make things **better**."

> [!tip] The Mindset Shift
> "It's not that we **have to** do this. We **get to** do this. There should be an **excitement** that we bring, an **enthusiasm** and a **hopefulness** to that process. That's how we're going to live **good lives** — the best life we can get."

**Conti:** Writing about what we know helps us **know it better** — because part of knowing something is also being aware that we **don't know everything** about it. When we organize our thoughts and try to put them down so other people can understand, we **learn** from that process.

## 02:07:25 — Closing Remarks

**Huberman's Key Takeaways:**

1. **Being on your own side** by seeing what you don't want to be controlled by — that's the essence of **agency**. Behavioral change is the hardest thing — and when people realize they're *not* changing, it's a double whammy.
2. **Focus on what's going right** — not to repeat the book title, but because it's **so vital**, especially in this time when the news is full of challenges. Focusing on what's going right, what **has** gone right — it's **essential** right now.

**Conti:** "We **get to** do this — and that's how we're going to live good lives. Maybe we get to the point where we can look back and feel good about the choices we've made — and feel okay about choices we've made even if they haven't led where we wanted — that we can still **embrace ourselves** and the lives we've led."
